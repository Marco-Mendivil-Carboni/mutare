\useblockstyle{TakeawayBlock}
\block{}{\emph{Takeaway:} The result of evolution in finite populations cannot be inferred solely from the average instantaneous growth rate $\langle\mu\rangle$: extinction also acts as a genuine selective pressure. In the large population limit, evolution selects the strategy that maximizes $\langle\mu\rangle$, whereas for smaller populations extinction risk biases evolution toward safer bet-hedging strategies.}

\useblockstyle{Block}

    \block{Introduction \& Objectives}{
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Natural populations face environments that change \emph{unpredictably} over time; when sensing is infeasible, bet-hedging offers a good alternative to adapt.
            \item Current understanding suggests populations evolve toward the strategy maximizing $\langle\mu\rangle$, but this is not an exact result for finite populations.
            \item \emph{Objective:} determine which bet-hedging strategy is actually favored by the process of natural selection.
        \end{itemize}
    }

    \block{Evolutionary Dynamics}{
        \begin{itemize}
            \item At any given time the population is effectively characterized by a single dominant strategy, with a narrow dispersion $\sigma_s$.
            \item Over longer times, advantageous mutations repeatedly arise and fix, abruptly shifting $\bar{s}(A)$ and driving a broad exploration of the strategy space.

    \block{Heuristic Interpretation}{
        \begin{itemize}
            \item The evolutionary dynamics can be modeled as a temporal concatenation of fixed-strategy regimes, weighted by the probability distribution of strategies $p(\bar{s})$ explored during evolution, which we approximate heuristically as:
                  \begin{equation*}
                      p(\bar{s})\propto\frac{\exp(N_\text{ini}\cdot\langle\mu\rangle(\bar{s}))}{r_\text{ext}(N_\text{ini},\bar{s})+p_\text{mut}\cdot\langle\mu_b\rangle(\bar{s})}
                  \end{equation*}
            \item The numerator reflects growth-driven selection, exponentially amplified over time, while the denominator accounts for the expected survival time before extinction or replacement by a fitter mutant.
            \item A fundamental scaling mismatch arises: growth enters \emph{exponentially} in $N_\text{ini}$, while extinction risk decays only \emph{algebraically} ($r_\text{ext}\sim N_\text{ini}^{-\alpha}$). This asymmetry determines the transition between growth-maximizing and safer strategies as the population size decreases.
        \end{itemize}
        \begin{center}
            \includegraphics[scale=2.5]{figures/asymmetric/fixed/exp_avg_avg_strat_phe_0.pdf}
            \includegraphics[scale=2.5]{figures/asymmetric/fixed/avg_avg_strat_phe_0.pdf}
            \captionof{figure}{\emph{Validation:} expected average strategy from the heuristic distribution (top) vs. observed average strategy from evolutive simulations (bottom), for different $N_\text{ini}$ and $p_\text{mut}$ values.}
        \end{center}

    \block{Discussion \& Conclusions}{
        \begin{itemize}
            \item We have verified that the transition from growth-maximizing strategies in large populations to safer bet-hedging strategies in small populations is robust to the mutation mechanism and model parameters, confirming that it is a fundamental feature of finite-population dynamics in fluctuating environments.
            \item Beyond theoretical modeling, designing experiments with bacterial populations to empirically test these predictions represents a promising next step.
        \end{itemize}
